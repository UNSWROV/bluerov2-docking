\subsection{Visual servoing and guidance for docking}
% IBVS vs PBVS (why PBVS suits a full pose pipeline). Behaviour-based state machines vs
% path planners for terminal docking. Servoing onto moving / compliant docks.

Visual servo control divides into image-based schemes, which regulate
feature error directly in the image plane, and position-based schemes,
which reconstruct a metric relative pose and regulate it in Cartesian
space \cite{chaumette2006visual}. When the perception layer already
produces a full 6-DOF pose from fiducial markers of known geometry
\cite{garrido2014automatic}, position-based servoing costs nothing extra
and keeps the docking geometry (standoff points, entry axes, capture
tolerances) expressible in physical units, which is why it is adopted
here.

A gap emerges around dock \emph{motion}. Deployed and proposed resident
docking stations are moored or suspended structures, and recent dynamic
analyses show they respond measurably to wave and current forcing through
their moorings \cite{guo2024dynamic}. Yet the docking-control literature
overwhelmingly treats the dock as fixed, with environmental disturbance
modelled at most as a steady current on the vehicle; the case of a dock
whose pose oscillates at wave frequencies, and of estimating and tracking
that motion from onboard vision alone, is comparatively unexplored.
Tracking a moving target from a moving observer additionally raises a
state-estimation question: a constant-velocity target model driven by
white process noise \cite{barshalom2001estimation}, implemented here as
an error-state Kalman filter \cite{sola2017quaternion}, assumes
measurement errors uncorrelated with the observer's own motion, an
assumption a vehicle-mounted camera can violate. Part~A of this work
quantifies both the envelope of docking against a moving dock and the
consequence of that violated assumption.
